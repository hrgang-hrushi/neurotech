The problem
Voice typing needs a voice.
What if you can’t use yours?
Dictation works, out loud
Tools like Wispr Flow turn speech into text, but only where you can speak.
Some places need silence
In a library or a meeting, speaking to your computer isn’t an option.
Some people can’t speak
For anyone who struggles to speak, a voice-first tool leaves them out entirely.
Our answer: silent jaw and tongue movements, read by an EEG cap, become words.

Why the hard part is hard
Every person
moves differently.
Volunteer B’s test showed decoding doesn’t yet carry over cleanly from one person to the next. These choices are how we close that gap.
Calibrate on one phrase
Send a known word such as PARIS once; it learns your dot length, thresholds and best channels.
A threshold per recording
Otsu’s method splits rest from movement for each recording, not one fixed number.
One movement, one symbol
Hysteresis, gap bridging and blip removal stop a single movement from chattering.
Human timing
It estimates each person’s unit and timing bias, so uneven dots and dashes still decode.
A forgiving decoder
Run-together or slightly wrong letters are repaired, and every repair is flagged.
It knows when it’s noise
A separability score warns when the decoded text is probably noise, instead of guessing.

Backup · Q&A
Is it the brain,
or a muscle?
Muscle, on purpose: the cap picks up jaw and tongue activity at the electrodes nearest the jaw.
That is a movement signal, the input the ANT track asked for, and it needs no sound.
We filter to 30–95 Hz, where muscle activity dominates a scalp recording.
Each session starts from a relaxed baseline, so movement stands out from rest.
Not yet reliable across users: Volunteer B’s run showed that, and we say so in the video.
A prototype on two team volunteers. We make no claim that it works for patients yet.
